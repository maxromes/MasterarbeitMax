Introduction Coral reefs are shaped by strong interactions between corals, algae, herbivorous fishes, predators, and physical disturbance. Herbivory can limit macroalgal accumulation and influence whether reefs remain coral-dominated or shift toward macroalgal states, but the strength of this process depends on herbivore identity, feeding mode, and local environmental context. ​ Herbivorous fishes are not an ecologically uniform group. Browsers can remove established, erect macroalgae, whereas grazers and scrapers often target turf or epilithic algal matrices. Across coral reefs, a small subset of species may account for a disproportionate share of macroalgal consumption, and the identity of the dominant consumers can vary among locations. This makes functional-group-specific monitoring important for understanding reef resilience. Baited Remote Underwater Video Systems (BRUVS) provide a non-extractive method for recording fish assemblages, but they do not sample the community neutrally. The bait plume, visual structure of the station, fish behaviour, current regime, and field of view jointly determine which individuals are detected. BRUVS therefore produce relative, bait-conditioned observations rather than absolute estimates of density. ​ Fish-based baits are effective for many predators, scavengers, and generalist carnivores, while plant-based treatments can alter the representation of herbivorous or omnivorous groups. Comparative experiments have shown that bait type may change assemblage structure and trophic composition even when broad species richness or total abundance changes little. The choice of bait is therefore not only a technical detail; it defines an ecological filter that can influence the conclusions drawn from a monitoring programme. This thesis tests whether macroalgal treatments complement conventional fish bait for recording herbivorous fishes and their feeding behaviour. It compares fish-based and macroalgal treatments in mono-BRUVS deployments around Mafia Island, Tanzania, and evaluates effects on assemblage composition, relative abundance measured as MaxN, herbivore representation, and observed feeding activity. Because no fish lengths were measured, the study does not estimate size structure, biomass, or absolute population density. 1.1 Study Region: Mafia Island Marine Park, Tanzania Mafia Island Marine Park is a managed coastal seascape in Tanzania in which ecological conditions and human use are closely linked. The park includes multiple management zones, reefs, villages, and adjacent islands, including Juani Island and the Chole Bay area. Its social and management context is relevant because fishing pressure, compliance, local resource dependence, and protection status can influence the baseline abundance and behaviour of reef fishes. ​ The study used three locations but two analytically distinct sampling components: the primary coral reef comparison at Milimani and Utumbi, and a separate lagoonal nursery experiment at Chole Nursery. Chole Nursery differed in habitat context, tidal accessibility, treatment availability, and replication structure. It is therefore treated as a second, site-specific experiment rather than as an interchangeable third reef replicate. This distinction is central to the interpretation of the Chole Nursery Acanthuridae and feeding results. The research question is particularly relevant in the Western Indian Ocean, where reef fish communities and their functional composition vary with environmental conditions and fishing pressure. Regional assessments show that surgeonfishes, parrotfishes, snappers, and wrasses are important components of East African reef fish communities, while vulnerable taxa can decline in areas exposed to human pressure. The experiment consequently focuses on the methodological problem of how bait choice changes the representation of these functional groups in BRUVS data. [FIGURE TO INSERT: Map of Mafia Island, Mafia Island Marine Park, Juani Island, and the three study sites Milimani, Utumbi, and Chole Nursery.] 1.2 From Extractive Methods to Remote Video Monitoring Historically, reef fish assemblages have been assessed using extractive techniques such as trawl nets, longlines, and traps. These methods can introduce species- and size-selective sampling, and bottom trawls may damage benthic habitats, whereas UVC can be constrained by depth and diver-related biases (Bond et al., 2022; Dunlop et al., 2015; Lindfield et al., 2014). Underwater Visual Census (UVC) is therefore valuable but does not provide a bias-free census of all reef fishes. These limitations have driven the adoption of remote underwater video over the past three decades (Mallet &amp; Pelletier, 2014; Logan et al., 2017). BRUVS are non-extractive, provide permanent records, can be replicated across habitats, and can be used beyond routine diving depths. Stereo systems additionally permit fish-length measurements through photogrammetry, although the mono-BRUVS used in this study did not provide that capability (Langlois et al., 2010; Langlois et al., 2020; Jessop et al., 2022). Large-scale surveys have demonstrated the capacity of BRUVS for ecosystem-level monitoring (Cappo et al., 2004). However, BRUVS introduce their own attraction and behavioural biases, so their data should be interpreted as method-specific observations rather than unbiased density estimates (Harvey et al., 2007; Whitmarsh et al., 2017). ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: Conceptual comparison of extractive sampling, UVC, and BRUVS, including their main strengths and limitations.] 1.3 Key Methodological Parameters The reliability of BRUVS data depends on a range of operational variables that are often incompletely reported. A global review of 161 studies identified 24 core parameters, including soak time, camera configuration, bait type and quantity, deployment depth, replicate spacing, and analysis software, that can fundamentally alter survey outcomes. The most critical parameters and their effects on data quality are synthesised in Section 1.6 below. [FIGURE TO INSERT: Overview diagram of the main BRUVS operational parameters and their potential effects on fish detection.] 1.4 Bait Type as a Determinant of Assemblage Representation Bait acts as the primary ecological filter in BRUVS surveys. Oily fish baits such as pilchards and mackerel can generate strong chemical plumes that attract piscivores and scavengers, but this selectivity can underrepresent species that respond primarily to visual cues or that feed on macroalgae, detritus, or invertebrates. ​ Comparative experiments on temperate and tropical reefs demonstrate that bait identity can shift the relative contribution of feeding guilds even when total species richness changes little. Fish-based baits tend to perform strongly for carnivorous and scavenging groups, whereas plant-based treatments can increase the representation of herbivores under particular environmental conditions. ​ These findings are important for coral reef monitoring because browsers and grazers do not contribute the same ecological functions. Standard fish bait should therefore not be assumed to provide a complete measure of herbivore communities, while macroalgal bait should not be treated as universally superior. A complementary design that matches bait type to the functional group of interest is more defensible. ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: Conceptual diagram of bait-plume dispersal and the expected trophic bias of fish-based versus macroalgal baits.] 1.5 Combining Methods for Comprehensive Assessment No single survey technique captures the full spectrum of reef fish diversity. Comparative studies consistently show that BRUVS and diver-based methods detect partly different assemblages, with BRUVS often recording more mobile predators and other bait-responsive taxa, whereas UVC or diver-operated video can detect more small, cryptic, and site-attached fishes. These complementary strengths reflect the fundamentally different sampling mechanisms: BRUVS attract mobile taxa via chemical cues, while UVC surveys the visually accessible community along a transect (Cheal et al., 2021; Schramm et al., 2020). ​ Integrated monitoring frameworks that combine BRUVS with UVC or diver-operated video yield more complete assemblage descriptions and stronger inference about habitat associations, protection effects, and functional diversity than either method alone (Logan et al., 2017). Where resources constrain multi-method designs, BRUVS remain a practical primary tool, particularly in depth-limited or safety-constrained settings, but only when the known trophic and behavioural biases of the bait are explicitly acknowledged in the study design and interpretation (Langlois et al., 2020). ​ 1.6 BRUVS Operational Parameters: A Synthesis The detailed BRUVS deployment settings, including soak time, bait quantity, camera configuration, spatial spacing, and abundance metrics, are reported in the Methods section. In this introduction, it is sufficient to note that these operational variables can strongly affect detection rates and should therefore be standardised and reported transparently. ​ 1.7 Research Aims and Hypotheses Building on the ecological and methodological context outlined above, this thesis pursues two interconnected aims: Aim 1. To quantify the effect of bait type (fish-based vs. macroalgal) on the species composition and abundance structure of BRUVS-recorded fish assemblages on coral reefs around Mafia Island. Aim 2. To determine whether macroalgal baits increase the detection and feeding activity of herbivorous fish families (Siganidae, Acanthuridae, Scaridae, Blenniidae) compared to standard fish-based baits. These aims translate into the following testable hypotheses: H1. Bait type significantly influences the overall species composition of BRUVS-recorded fish assemblages, as detected by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) on Jaccard distances. H2. Macroalgal baits yield higher MaxN values for herbivorous fish families (Siganidae, Acanthuridae, Scaridae, Blenniidae) than fish-based baits, particularly at sites with intact herbivore populations. H3. Herbivorous fishes exhibit higher feeding activity (proportion of deployment time with feeding behaviour) at macroalgal bait stations compared to fish-based bait stations. 2. Methods 2.1 Study Sites and Experimental Components Three study locations were used around Mafia Island, but they represent two analytically distinct experimental components. The primary reef component comprised Milimani and Utumbi , which are coral reef monitoring sites sampled under broadly comparable high-tide conditions. The second component comprised Chole Nursery , a lagoonal site close to an active coral nursery. Chole Nursery was treated as a separate, site-specific experiment rather than as a fully interchangeable third reef replicate. The Chole Nursery component differed from the reef component in habitat context, tidal accessibility, treatment availability, and sampling structure. It therefore provides an ecologically informative case study of bait response in a lagoonal nursery setting, but it should not be interpreted as a direct replication of the Milimani and Utumbi comparison. Results involving Chole Nursery are consequently presented and discussed separately wherever the analysis permits. Cross-site analyses are used to describe overall assemblage structure, while the primary bait comparisons are interpreted within the relevant experimental component. The main fieldwork period ran from 25 October 2024 to 10 December 2024. Additional BRUVS videos recorded by Nicole Schröter during her doctoral research were collected outside this main period, including both earlier and later dates. Their original dates are retained in Appendix A: Deployment Schedule. ​ At all sites, BRUVS units were deployed at fixed positions with a consistent orientation to standardise sampling effort. At Utumbi and Milimani, deployments were conducted at high tide to ensure comparable water depths and current conditions. At Chole Nursery, deployments were conducted at both high and low tide depending on the treatment, reflecting the site's tidal accessibility. Water depths ranged from 4.5 to 6.5 m at Utumbi, 3.5 to 6.0 m at Milimani, and 3.0 to 5.3 m at Chole Nursery. A 4-litre jerrycan served as both camera housing and ballast weight, connected to a metal frame to which the bait basket was attached. The camera was oriented horizontally relative to the seabed. Results The Results are organised around two analytical components. Milimani and Utumbi constitute the primary coral reef comparison, whereas Chole Nursery is presented as a separate lagoonal nursery experiment because its habitat, tidal accessibility, treatment portfolio, and replication structure differed. Global analyses including all three locations are reported for overall assemblage structure, but Chole Nursery-specific bait and feeding results are interpreted separately rather than as a direct third replication of the reef experiment. 3.1 Core Results 3.1.1 Overall Bait Effects on Assemblage Composition, H1 Hypothesis H1 predicted that bait type would influence the overall composition of BRUVS-recorded fish assemblages. ​ Global PERMANOVA results supported this prediction within both experimental components: Milimani ($p = 0.0242$), Utumbi ($p = 0.0046$), and Chole Nursery ($p = 0.0016$). The Milimani and Utumbi values describe the primary reef comparison, whereas the Chole Nursery value describes the separate lagoonal nursery experiment. Pairwise comparisons were generally no longer significant after Holm correction, indicating that the global compositional response was distributed across several bait relationships rather than being attributable to one universally distinct pair. At Utumbi, the Control and Sargassum treatments showed a Jaccard overlap of 0.764, while at Chole Nursery the highest overlap was observed between Algae Strings and Algae Mix, with a Jaccard value of 0.662. ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: PERMANOVA ordination or equivalent multivariate plot showing bait-associated assemblage differences by site.] 3.1.2 Taxon-Specific Bait Effects The taxon-specific MaxN analysis provided a more conservative picture than the global PERMANOVA. Across the three sites, 104 taxa were tested at Milimani, 99 at Chole Nursery, and 120 at Utumbi. Six, eleven, and eight taxa, respectively, showed uncorrected bait signals, but none remained significant after Holm correction. These results do not provide robust evidence for a consistent bait effect on individual taxa when all taxa are tested simultaneously. The absence of Holm-robust taxon-specific effects does not contradict the global PERMANOVA. It indicates that the overall compositional shift was distributed across several taxa and was not driven by a small number of individually strong responses. [FIGURE TO INSERT: Forest plot or heatmap of taxon-specific bait effects, including effect direction and corrected significance.] 3.1.3 Standard Fish-versus-Algae Functional Comparison The standard functional comparison contrasted fish-based and algal bait treatments across broader dietary and taxonomic feature groups. Several corrected differences were detected, especially at Utumbi, but the significant effects were predominantly directed towards higher values in fish-baited deployments. Robust examples included wrasses at Milimani and Utumbi, eels at Utumbi, and broader invertebrate- or predator-related groups. Cliff's Delta values ranged from 0.800 to 0.978, and all reported examples were directed towards fish greater than algae. ​ This pattern indicates trophic filtering by bait. At the level of the broad functional comparison, the direction was not consistent with the prediction that macroalgal bait would generally increase herbivore representation. Fish-based bait remained more effective for several fish-oriented and invertivore-associated groups. ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: Functional-group comparison plot contrasting fish-based and algal bait responses.] 3.1.4 A Priori Herbivore MaxN Analysis, H2 Hypothesis H2 predicted higher MaxN values for herbivorous fish families at macroalgal bait stations than at fish-baited stations. The a priori test focused on Siganidae, Acanthuridae, Scaridae, and Blenniidae. ​ The strongest support for H2 was found at Chole Nursery for Acanthuridae. Median MaxN was 22.0 in algal treatments and 4.5 in fish treatments. This difference remained significant after Holm correction ($p_ = 0.0070$, $p_ = 0.0278$, Cliff's Delta = 1.0). Siganidae at Chole Nursery showed the same direction, but the effect did not remain significant after correction. At Utumbi, Scaridae and Siganidae also showed directional differences favouring algal bait, but neither comparison was statistically robust after Holm correction. Milimani provided no support for the predicted algal effect, with Acanthuridae showing higher median MaxN under fish bait. Overall, the evidence for H2 was concentrated in Acanthuridae at Chole Nursery and was strongly site-dependent. ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: Boxplots or raincloud plots of herbivore MaxN by bait type, family, and study site.] 3.1.5 Herbivore Feeding Activity, H3 Hypothesis H3 predicted higher feeding activity by herbivorous fishes at macroalgal bait stations than at fish-baited stations. The feeding analysis addressed active interaction with the bait and therefore tested a behavioural response distinct from MaxN. ​ At Chole Nursery, the feeding rate was 0.2038 under algal bait and 0.0000 under fish bait. The difference remained significant after Holm correction ($$p_{raw} = 0.005709$$, $$p_{Holm} = 0.017126$$, Cliff's Delta = 1.0). At Milimani and Utumbi, feeding rates were directionally higher under algal bait, but neither comparison was statistically supported. The detailed feeding-filter results showed the same direction at Chole Nursery but did not remain significant after Holm correction ($$p_{raw} = 0.0112$$, $$p_{Holm} = 0.218$$, Cliff's Delta = 1.0). These feeding-filter results are therefore interpreted as secondary evidence within the Chole Nursery component. 3.1.6 Behavioural Comparison Beyond Feeding The interested category was analysed separately from feeding because attraction to the bait does not necessarily indicate bait consumption or attempted consumption. Feeding produced more pronounced bait-related signals than interest. The targeted Chole Nursery comparison between Algae Mix and Mackerel showed strong separation for two predefined taxa, but the result remained just below the Holm-adjusted significance threshold. ​ [FIGURE TO INSERT: Comparison of feeding and interested behaviours by bait type and site.] 3.2 Additional Results and Context 3.2.1 Dominant Site Effects The strongest statistical structure in the dataset was the difference among study sites. Discussion 4.1 Main interpretation of the study The central result is that bait type changed the assemblage detected by the BRUVS, but the magnitude and direction of the response depended strongly on site and taxonomic or behavioural group. Global PERMANOVA results were significant at Milimani ($p = 0.0242$), Utumbi ($p = 0.0046$), and Chole Nursery ($p = 0.0016$). This supports H1 at the assemblage level, but the three site results should not be interpreted as three equivalent replicates of one experiment. Milimani and Utumbi form the primary reef comparison, whereas Chole Nursery represents a separate lagoonal nursery experiment with a different habitat, tidal regime, treatment portfolio, and replication structure. The most defensible interpretation is therefore that bait type altered the bait-conditioned observation process within each experimental context, rather than that one universal bait effect operated identically across all locations. The absence of Holm-significant taxon-specific effects, despite significant global assemblage tests, is not a contradiction. Six, eleven, and eight taxa produced uncorrected bait signals at Milimani, Chole Nursery, and Utumbi, respectively, but none remained significant after Holm correction. The multivariate signal was therefore distributed across several taxa with modest responses, rather than being driven by one universally responsive species. This pattern is compatible with the bait-specific and species-specific responses reported in comparative BRUVS studies, where bait treatments can produce detectable changes in assemblage structure while individual taxa respond inconsistently among locations. ​ The interpretation of the PERMANOVA should nevertheless remain conditional until the analysis reports the corresponding test of homogeneity of multivariate dispersions. PERMANOVA can reflect differences in group centroids, differences in within-group dispersion, or both. Published BRUVS studies therefore commonly use PERMDISP alongside PERMANOVA before attributing a significant result to a shift in assemblage position rather than to unequal variability among bait treatments. If PERMDISP was not performed in the present analysis, this should be added as a limitation and the results should be described as bait-associated compositional differences rather than as definitive centroid shifts. 4.2 Trophic filtering and comparison with previous bait studies The direction of the broader functional comparison provides a clear ecological explanation for part of the assemblage response. Several corrected effects favoured fish-based bait, including wrasses, eels, and other fish-oriented or invertivore-associated groups, with large Cliff's Delta values. This is consistent with the established role of oily fish bait as a strong attractant for predators, scavengers, generalist carnivores, and some invertebrate feeders. Harvey et al. found that bait increased predatory and scavenging fishes without necessarily excluding herbivorous or omnivorous fishes, while also improving the discrimination of habitat-related assemblage differences. The present fish-bait signal should therefore not be interpreted simply as a failure of the experiment. It demonstrates that the conventional bait remains informative for a different component of the fish community. Comparative bait experiments also show why a universal ranking of bait types would be misleading. Wraith et al. reported strong effects of bait type on species richness and feeding-guild representation, but little overall effect on relative abundance, and they observed substantial spatial variation among locations. Pilchards were the only bait that consistently attracted herbivores in their study, while crushed urchin performed poorly. In the Persian Gulf, animal-based bait attracted more species and carnivores, whereas a plant-based bait produced the highest herbivore abundance; nevertheless, broad species composition was comparable among bait treatments. These comparisons closely match the present results: the treatment effect is strongest when the response is considered at the level of trophic or behavioural composition, whereas species-level effects are more heterogeneous. The fish-bait advantage for wrasses, eels, and invertivore-associated groups is biologically plausible because these taxa commonly use chemical cues to locate animal-derived food or prey disturbed by feeding activity. It may also reflect secondary attraction. Fish approaching and feeding at the bait can attract conspecifics or other predators, so some individuals recorded during fish-bait deployments may have responded to the activity of other fish rather than to the bait itself. This mechanism has been documented in BRUVS work as conspecific attraction and attraction to commotion around the station. It is especially relevant for interpreting the category "interested", which records attention to the station but does not prove bait consumption. 4.3 Conditional support for H2: herbivore detection H2 was supported only conditionally and primarily within the Chole Nursery experiment. Acanthuridae had a median MaxN of 22.0 under algal treatments compared with 4.5 under fish treatments, and the difference remained significant after Holm correction ($p_ = 0.0278$, Cliff's Delta = 1.0). The same direction was observed for Siganidae at Chole Nursery and for some comparisons at Utumbi, but these effects were not robust after correction. At Milimani, the direction was reversed for Acanthuridae. The result should therefore be interpreted as evidence for a site-specific response in the Chole Nursery experiment, not as general superiority of macroalgal bait for all herbivores or as a uniformly replicated reef-wide effect. One explanation is that the algal treatment matched the local foraging ecology of surgeonfishes more closely than mackerel did. Herbivorous fishes are not a single functional unit. Browsers consume larger erect macroalgae, whereas grazers and scrapers focus more strongly on epilithic algal matrices, turf, or algae associated with the substrate. An algal bait composed of Ulva and Sargassum may therefore be attractive to some browsing or generalist herbivores while being less relevant to species that normally crop turf, scrape carbonate substrate, feed on epiphytes, or select algae according to chemical or nutritional quality. Food identity alone may not explain the Chole Nursery response. The physical presentation of the bait differed between treatments. Algae Strings were positioned vertically in the water column, whereas the other basket treatments were presented in a confined cylinder. This creates a potential treatment difference in visual profile, movement, accessibility, and exposure to the current in addition to a difference in chemical composition. The current design therefore cannot isolate a purely chemical preference for macroalgae unless the final analysis is restricted to treatments with comparable presentation or the design is explicitly described as a comparison of complete bait packages. A second explanation is local resource context. Herbivores may respond more strongly to an offered algal resource when preferred forage is scarce, patchy, or locally depleted, but may ignore an experimental bait when similar or more profitable algae are abundant nearby. Foraging studies show that herbivore preferences depend on the form, quality, availability, and yield of algal food, and that closely related fishes can exploit the same reef habitat in different ways. The Chole Nursery result may therefore indicate that algal bait was locally salient at that site rather than that all reef herbivores have a general preference for the deployed algae. A third explanation is that the site contained a particular combination of herbivore abundance, habitat structure, and predator pressure. Macroalgae-consuming fishes can forage socially, and group feeding can increase feeding rates by reducing vigilance and facilitating the discovery of patchily distributed resources. If Chole Nursery supported groups of Acanthuridae or provided suitable approach routes, a single early visitor could have increased the probability of subsequent visits. This would elevate both MaxN and feeding observations without implying a proportional increase in the underlying population size. The absence of a similar response at Milimani and Utumbi should not be treated simply as a failed replication of Chole Nursery. These two reef sites constitute the primary comparison, while Chole Nursery was designed and sampled as a separate second experiment in a lagoonal nursery context. Differences among the components may reflect the local species pool, background algal availability, reef exposure, current regime, predator activity, tidal state, treatment combinations, and sampling structure. Chole Nursery had only 11 deployments, including three Algae Strings, three Algae Mix, one Control, and four Mackerel deployments, whereas Milimani and Utumbi had different treatment portfolios and more deployments overall. The Chole Nursery result is therefore a strong hypothesis-generating observation for this habitat context, while the Milimani and Utumbi results provide the primary reef comparison. Neither component alone supports a universal conclusion about macroalgal bait across all Mafia Island reef habitats. H3 provides a more direct test of bait interaction than MaxN. At Chole Nursery, the feeding rate was 0.2038 under algal bait and 0.0000 under fish bait, with a Holm-adjusted $$p_{raw} = 0.005709$$ and $$p_{Holm} = 0.017126$$, Cliff's Delta = 1.0. This pattern indicates that the algal treatment was associated with active feeding behaviour, not only with the presence of herbivores near the camera. It is the strongest evidence in the dataset for a functional match between an algal treatment and the behaviour of at least part of the local herbivore assemblage, but it belongs specifically to the Chole Nursery experiment. The distinction between presence, interest, and feeding is essential. A fish may enter the field of view while moving between habitats, investigate the frame, follow another fish, defend a territory, or exploit invertebrates associated with the bait basket without consuming the intended bait. Remote video foraging studies show that fish abundance alone can be a poor proxy for herbivory because a small number of species or individuals may account for most bites and because interactions can vary among sites. The behavioural result should therefore be presented as evidence of treatment-associated feeding observations, not as a direct estimate of total herbivory or algal removal from the reef. The operational definition of feeding was applied strictly: visible contact with the bait was required, together with actual uptake or biting of the bait. Individuals feeding simultaneously were annotated separately, following the same individual-level procedure used for MaxN. Feeding-event duration was not measured, so the feeding variable represents the occurrence or recorded number of feeding interactions rather than time spent feeding. All videos were annotated by the author, Max Romes. Quality control was supported by expert review and discussion, particularly with Nicole Schröter, and the practice video was independently annotated by both observers before analysis. Because the primary observer was not blinded to treatment identity, the possibility of observer-related classification bias should still be acknowledged. The absence of corrected feeding effects at Milimani and Utumbi does not necessarily refute the mechanism. Feeding activity is sensitive to time of day, motivation, local prey availability, predator risk, social context, and the nutritional or chemical properties of the bait. In macroalgal systems, feeding rates can vary substantially from day to day even when the same dominant herbivore species is present, and the identity of the principal consumer can change across locations. The results therefore support a context-dependent behavioural response rather than a uniform effect across all reef environments. 4.5 Fish behaviour around the BRUVS BRUVS do not record a neutral cross-section of fish passing through space. Individuals differ in sensory ability, hunger, activity rhythm, boldness, swimming capacity, schooling tendency, territoriality, and willingness to approach an unfamiliar object. These differences determine whether a fish detects the plume, follows it to the station, enters the field of view, remains near the bait, and reaches the point at which it can be classified as feeding. ​ The fish-bait signal may include several behavioural pathways. Carnivores and scavengers can respond directly to dissolved animal-derived cues. Other fishes may be attracted to suspended particles released from the basket, to the visual contrast of the bait, or to the concentration of prey around the station. Still others may be drawn by the movement and feeding activity of the first arrivals. Published BRUVS studies explicitly distinguish bait attraction from curiosity, random movement, social attraction, and predatory behaviour around the station. ​ Predator-mediated avoidance is a plausible explanation for some differences between treatments. Large predators or aggressive individuals may dominate access to the bait and deter smaller or subordinate fishes. Agonistic interactions have been shown to increase with fish density and to cause subordinate fishes, which are often smaller, to leave the camera field. Consequently, a low MaxN or low feeding rate under one bait treatment may reflect exclusion or risk-sensitive behaviour rather than low attraction to the bait itself. This mechanism is particularly important at Chole Nursery, where an algal bait may attract herbivores but also create a local feeding aggregation. If predator presence was lower, if structural refuges were closer, or if the bait was presented in a way that allowed rapid escape, herbivores may have remained longer and fed more often. Conversely, at a more exposed reef site, the same bait could be detected but not approached because the perceived predation risk exceeded its nutritional value. Hoey and Bellwood showed that herbivorous fishes avoided dense stands of canopy-forming macroalgae, probably because those structures altered perceived predation risk rather than simply increasing food availability. The physical appearance of the experimental algal treatment should therefore be considered part of the behavioural stimulus. The category "interested" is useful for detecting these intermediate responses, but it should not be collapsed into feeding. A strong interest response with little feeding may indicate curiosity, inspection, social attraction, or a mismatch between the cue and the fish's actual diet. Conversely, feeding can occur with few individuals if a small number of specialists account for most interactions. The manuscript's decision to analyse feeding separately is therefore conceptually appropriate, but the category definitions and event-level data should be reported in enough detail to support replication. 4.6 Bait plume dynamics, currents, and tide Hydrodynamics provide a mechanistic explanation for why the same bait can perform differently among deployments. Current speed and direction determine the distance, shape, dilution, and persistence of the chemical signal. Taylor et al. measured current velocities from 0.02 to 0.65 m s−1 and found that estimated bait-plume areas could vary by orders of magnitude, changing the effective sampling effort of BRUV deployments. Their results show that current can have two simultaneous effects: it changes the area from which fish may detect the bait, and it can independently influence habitat selection and fish activity. The implications for this study are substantial. A stronger current may extend the plume and attract mobile predators from a wider area, but it can also dilute or advect plant-derived cues rapidly away from the camera. A weak current may retain cues close to the station and favour site-attached or slower-moving fishes, but it may also reduce the total distance over which a bait is detectable. The direction of the plume may further determine whether fish approach from visible downstream routes or remain hidden behind reef structure. These mechanisms could produce bait-by-site differences even when bait mass and camera settings are standardised. Tidal state is not equivalent to current speed. High and low tide can be associated with different flow conditions, depth, light, habitat access, and fish activity, but a tide label alone does not quantify the local hydrodynamic environment. Studies of BRUVS in dynamic coastal waters recommend measuring current velocity and considering a measure of plume area or spatial sampling effort because tide-associated changes can alter the conclusions drawn from MaxN and assemblage data. The present study used high tide at Milimani and Utumbi but both high and low tide at Chole Nursery. Tide is therefore a potential confounder of the site-specific Chole Nursery result and should be included explicitly in the limitations unless current measurements or a balanced design are available. The bait basket itself may also modify plume formation. The metal mesh, cylindrical shape, 1 cm mesh size, approximately half-filled volume, and the distinction between basket-based treatments and vertical Algae Strings determine how water moves through the bait and how particles are released. The BRUVS literature identifies bait preparation, bait quantity, deployment method, current measurement, and replicate spacing as continuing methodological gaps. The present manuscript should therefore avoid attributing treatment differences exclusively to taxonomy or palatability when the physical release regime differed among treatments. 4.7 Site effects, habitat, and visibility Site was the dominant source of biological structure in the dataset. Of 161 tested taxa, 93 were significant before correction and 36 remained significant after Holm correction. Humpback snapper were concentrated at Chole Nursery, whereas soldierfish were concentrated at Utumbi. These differences are too extensive to treat site as a minor nuisance factor. They support the decision to interpret the Milimani-Utumbi reef comparison separately from the Chole Nursery lagoonal experiment, because bait effects operate on different ecological baselines. The site result is consistent with BRUVS studies showing that habitat, depth, structural complexity, hydrodynamics, and protection status can shape assemblage composition more strongly than a single methodological treatment. Fish assemblages in different habitats can remain partly overlapping while still being clearly distinguishable, and spatial differences can occur over scales of only a few kilometres. The short distance between Milimani and Utumbi does not make them interchangeable replicates, particularly because the two sites may differ in coral structure, nursery influence, exposure, or local fish movement. Chole Nursery should also be interpreted as a different habitat context rather than merely as a third reef site. Its lagoonal setting, tidal accessibility, low-tide deployments, and proximity to a coral nursery can influence both fish composition and behaviour. Habitat connectivity may allow fishes to move between nursery, lagoonal, seagrass, mangrove, and reef environments, creating assemblages that combine resident species with transient visitors. BRUVS can detect these habitat-linked differences, but the bait plume may also attract individuals from adjacent habitats, making the spatial origin of a recorded fish uncertain. ​ Visibility was positively associated with species richness and the video-level MaxN peak in the bivariate analyses, with $$\rho = 0.563$$ and $$q = 0.000138$$ for richness, and $$\rho = 0.467$$ and $$q = 0.00161$$ for the MaxN peak. These relationships are plausible because poor visibility reduces the probability that fish are seen, identified, or counted at a distance. However, the associations disappeared after controlling for site and bait, and the additional adjusted analyses supported the null result. Visibility should therefore be retained as a quality-control variable and reported transparently, but it should not be presented as the primary explanation for the bait effects. 4.8 Methodological implications for BRUVS monitoring The results argue for complementary rather than replacement sampling. Fish-based bait remains useful for detecting predatory, scavenging, and invertivore-associated groups, while macroalgal treatments may add information about selected herbivores and their active feeding behaviour. This conclusion is consistent with comparative surveys in which BRUVS and transect-based methods sampled partly different assemblages, with BRUVS recording more mobile predators and transect methods recording more territorial, cryptic, or site-attached fishes. ​ A two-bait design can therefore be justified when the monitoring objective is functional coverage rather than a single index of total fish abundance. The design should, however, distinguish between a factorial comparison of bait chemistry and a comparison of complete deployment packages. In the present study, Mackerel, basket-based algal treatments, Algae Strings, Algae Mix, Gut Weed, and Control do not necessarily represent equivalent physical treatments. Control was completely unbaited. These definitions must be used consistently in all pooled and site-specific analyses. The use of MaxN is defensible as a conservative relative-abundance metric because it reduces repeated counting of individuals that revisit the station. It is not an estimate of absolute density, biomass, or the total number of individuals that visited during the video. Studies using individual identification show that MaxN can underestimate the number of distinct individuals, particularly when multiple individuals visit sequentially. The present thesis should consequently use terms such as "relative abundance", "recorded abundance", or "maximum observed number" and should avoid "population size", "density", or "biomass" unless additional calibration data are available. The standardised 43-minute analysis window improves comparability among the videos, but one supplementary Chole Nursery deployment lasted only 32 minutes and was treated as a predefined special case. All 46 videos were included in the statistical dataset unless a specific analysis was explicitly described otherwise. The 32-minute video was included in some analyses and examined separately in others because it was the only deployment of this duration, was conducted at Chole Nursery, and had poor visibility. The Results and statistical methods must identify clearly for each analysis whether this video was included, excluded, or analysed separately. ​ 4.9 Limitations and future research The first limitation is the two-component structure of the study. Milimani and Utumbi constitute the primary coral reef comparison, whereas Chole Nursery was conducted as a separate lagoonal nursery experiment. Treatment replication is uneven within and between these components, some treatments occur at only one site, and Chole Nursery was sampled under a different tidal regime. The robust Acanthuridae and feeding results at Chole Nursery are therefore valuable for that specific habitat context, but they cannot be interpreted as a fully replicated reef-wide bait effect. All 46 videos were included in the statistical analysis unless a specific result states otherwise. The supplementary videos were collected outside the main fieldwork period and may differ in season, weather, camera generation, bait preparation, fish condition, and deployment history. The 32-minute Chole Nursery video was treated as a special case because it was the only deployment of that duration and had poor visibility; it was included in some analyses and evaluated separately in others. This distinction must be reported explicitly for every analysis rather than described as a general exclusion of supplementary videos. The third limitation is incomplete environmental characterisation. Visibility, depth, temperature, tide, cloud, sun, wind, and rain are reported, but the current speed and direction, rugosity, benthic composition, relief, and exact distance between replicate deployments are not yet documented in the manuscript. These omissions matter because plume area, fish approach routes, habitat use, and predator risk all depend on them. ​ The fourth limitation concerns behavioural annotation. Feeding, interest, and disinterest were recorded as comments rather than as a fully specified event-based protocol. The thesis should report observer training, whether treatment identity was visible to the observer, inter-observer agreement beyond the practice video, the time resolution of annotations, and the rule used when several fish interacted simultaneously. The use of an independent second annotator or blinded re-annotation of a random subset would strengthen confidence in the behavioural results. The fifth limitation is taxonomic and ecological resolution. Sea lettuce and Gut Weed are currently identified only as morphologically distinct Ulva spp., and the species identity, palatability, chemical composition, nutritional value, and storage-related changes of the algal baits were not measured. Macroalgal consumers can be highly selective, and native algae may be preferred over chemically defended or unfamiliar taxa. The interpretation should therefore refer to the tested algal treatments rather than to macroalgae as a homogeneous category. Future experiments should use balanced factorial designs with treatment combinations replicated across multiple reef and lagoon sites, while standardising tide or directly measuring current speed and direction. Chemical bait type, physical presentation, bait mass, bait volume, moisture, freshness, storage duration, and release regime should be recorded separately. A factorial comparison of fish bait, individual algal species, mixed algae, and visually matched controls would help distinguish chemical attraction from visual structure and texture. Future work should also add unbaited or sham-baited controls, stereo cameras or calibrated mono-camera length proxies where size is required, and a second camera outside the bait field of view to detect exclusion by aggressive fish. Replicate spacing should be justified from local hydrodynamics and fish movement rather than from an arbitrary distance. In addition, day and night deployments or time-of-day covariates would help separate bait preference from activity rhythms. 4.10 Overall conclusion The study provides evidence that bait type changes the fish assemblage and that macroalgal treatments can increase the recording of Acanthuridae and active herbivore feeding under particular conditions at Chole Nursery. The evidence does not support replacing fish bait universally or claiming that macroalgal bait improves herbivore detection at all sites. The strongest conclusion is methodological: BRUVS bait acts as an ecological filter, and a monitoring design that combines complementary bait treatments, explicit behavioural annotation, and site-specific environmental covariates can provide a more functionally informative view of reef fish communities than a single conventional bait alone. GoPro HERO 8, 9, and 10 cameras. The cameras recorded sound, but audio was removed during video processing before annotation. The video clips were merged into deployment files, speed and colour were not altered, and the resulting videos were uploaded to BIIGLE for annotation. Annotation Process All annotations were performed using BIIGLE. Two key types of annotations were used: First Seen. When a species was spotted for the first time, it was annotated. If multiple individuals appeared simultaneously, they were all marked as "first seen." A short time window of a few seconds was used to define this event. MaxN (Maximum Number). This annotation recorded the highest number of individuals of a species in a single frame. Any individuals appearing between the "first seen" event and the MaxN event were not annotated. Identification Process To ensure accuracy, a practice video was annotated separately by both the researcher and Nicole Schröter. The results had to match. Ongoing communication with Nicole Schröter was maintained for quality control. The goal was to identify all individuals at the species level. If species identification was not possible, the next higher taxonomic level was used, following the same annotation rules (First Seen, MaxN). Identification resources included iNaturalist, WORMS, Indo-Pacific tropical reef fish literature, and consultation with Nicole Schröter. Handling Unclear Identifications Due to the complex nature of coral reefs, the background often contained blurry or indistinct fish, which were not annotated. The threshold for when a fish was too blurry or distant to be identified was decided in consultation with Nicole Schröter. Fish in the foreground that could not be identified immediately were temporarily marked as "unidentified" and could potentially be identified later. Behavioural Categories Feeding. A fish was classified as feeding only when visible contact with the bait and actual uptake or biting of the bait were observed. Individuals feeding simultaneously were annotated separately, following the individual-level procedure used for MaxN. Three behavioural categories were added to the annotations: Feeding. Fish actively trying to eat the bait or attempting to access it were classified as "feeding." Interested. Fish that circled the bait basket curiously, revisited the bait, or were attracted by other fish near the bait were marked as "interested." Behavioural annotations (Feeding, Interested) were treated as comments rather than primary taxonomic annotations. Feeding-event duration was not measured. Feeding and interest were recorded separately from MaxN and First Seen for each species, and simultaneously feeding individuals were annotated separately using the same individual-level procedure as MaxN. All videos were annotated by Max Romes. Quality control was provided through expert review, particularly consultation with Nicole Schröter, and through independent annotation of a practice video by both observers before the main annotation. Environmental factors such as strong currents or poor visibility were noted separately. Uninterested. Fish that swam past the bait basket without showing interest, stayed far away, or were territorial species associated with surrounding corals were marked as "uninterested." Uninterested fish were annotated without extra behavioural information, making their presence comparable across different videos from the same site. Additional Notes Behavioural annotations (Feeding, Interested) were considered comments rather than primary annotations. These behaviours were recorded separately from MaxN and First Seen for the species. For example: MaxN Green Damsel = 9, MaxN Green Damsel Interested = 3, MaxN Green Damsel Feeding = 2.
